# Posting kit

## Every upload
1. Upload the MP4 from `episodes/<folder>/`.
2. Paste the caption below.
3. **More options → turn on "AI-generated content"** (the voice is AI).
4. Optional: add a trending sound at very low volume (roughly 5–10%) so it doesn't fight the voice.
5. Cover: pick a frame where the big title text is visible.
6. After posting, pin a comment that asks for the next topic (drives replies, and replies to comments make easy videos).

## Suggested order
Post the pilot first, then one per day. Have the next one ready before the current one runs out.

## Fact sources (for replying to "source?" comments)
- **Knuckles:** Kawchuk et al., *PLOS ONE* (2015): real-time MRI of joint cracking (bubble formation). Donald Unger, 2009 Ig Nobel Prize in Medicine: 60 years of cracking one hand only.
- **Brain freeze:** brain tissue has no pain receptors; cold on the palate changes blood flow, and the trigeminal nerve refers the pain to the forehead (Serrador et al., 2012, FASEB meeting presentation).
- **Gum:** gum base is indigestible but passes through the gut in days; blockages reported only in children who swallowed large amounts habitually (Milov et al., *Pediatrics*, 1998).
- **Tickling:** the cerebellum predicts the sensory result of your own movements and dampens it (Blakemore, Wolpert & Frith, *Nature Neuroscience*, 1998). With a self-controlled tickle robot, adding a 100–300 ms delay made it feel more ticklish (Blakemore, Frith & Wolpert, *Journal of Cognitive Neuroscience*, 1999).
- **Funny bone:** the ulnar nerve passes through the cubital tunnel behind the medial epicondyle of the humerus, covered only by skin and fat; it supplies the little finger and half of the ring finger. The humerus/"humorous" pun is a popular explanation for the name, not a proven origin.
- **Hiccups:** involuntary spasm of the diaphragm followed by sudden closure of the glottis (the "hic"). Common triggers: large or fast meals, carbonated drinks, swallowing air. Fetal hiccups are seen on ultrasound. Charles Osborne holds the Guinness record: hiccupping from 1922 to 1990 (68 years). Hiccups lasting over 48 hours ("persistent hiccups") warrant medical evaluation.
- **Goosebumps:** arrector pili muscles attached to hair follicles contract under sympathetic nervous system activation (cold, fear, strong emotion), raising the hairs and the skin around them. In furry mammals this traps insulating air and makes the animal look bigger.
- **Leg falling asleep:** sustained pressure compresses a nerve (and its blood supply), disrupting signal transmission and causing numbness; when pressure is released the nerve resumes firing irregularly, producing "pins and needles" (transient paresthesia). Frequent or unexplained episodes warrant medical evaluation.
- **Stomach growling:** borborygmi are sounds from gas and fluid moved by gut contractions (peristalsis); they're louder with an empty gut. In the fasting state the migrating motor complex sweeps the stomach and small intestine roughly every 90–120 minutes.
- **Myth or Fact (ep 10):** 10% brain myth: imaging shows activity across the whole brain over a day. Taller in the morning: spinal discs compress during the day (about 1 cm). Sneezing doesn't stop the heart; pressure changes can briefly alter rhythm. Gastric acid (pH ~1.5–3.5) corrodes some metals such as zinc. Bacteria-to-human cell ratio is roughly 1:1 (Sender, Fuchs & Milo, *PLOS Biology*, 2016). Human ultra-weak photon emission ~1000× below what eyes can see, imaged with ultra-sensitive cameras (Kobayashi et al., *PLOS ONE*, 2009).
- **Sleep 30 days (ep 11):** sleep increases clearance of brain waste via the glymphatic system (Xie et al., *Science*, 2013, in mice). Sleep supports memory consolidation. Short sleep raises ghrelin and lowers leptin (Spiegel et al., *Annals of Internal Medicine*, 2004; Taheri et al., *PLOS Medicine*, 2004). Sleep deprivation increases amygdala reactivity (Yoo et al., *Current Biology*, 2007). The week-by-week framing is a storytelling device; these effects come from regular sleep, not a fixed schedule.
- **Gross edition (ep 12):** no evidence for "swallowing spiders in your sleep" (the oft-quoted statistic has no source). Mucus is ~90–95% water. Nails don't grow after death; dehydrating skin retracts. A large share of house dust is tracked/blown in from outdoors (e.g. Layton & Beamer, *Environmental Science & Technology*, 2009); shed skin contributes. Earwax is produced by ceruminous glands (modified apocrine sweat glands) plus sebaceous secretions and dead skin. The stomach protects itself with mucus and bicarbonate; surface lining cells are replaced every few days.
- **Kitchen sponge (ep 13):** up to 5.4 × 10^10 bacterial cells per cm³ in used kitchen sponges; regularly "sanitized" sponges did not have lower loads; authors suggest replacing sponges weekly (Cardinale et al., *Scientific Reports*, 2017).
- **Stomach (ep 14):** gastric acid (pH ~1.5–3.5); the mucus-bicarbonate layer neutralizes acid at the epithelial surface; surface epithelial cells are replaced every few days (≈3–5); a breakdown of this barrier leads to peptic ulcers.
- **Farts (ep 15):** healthy adults pass gas roughly 14–23 times a day; most intestinal gas is swallowed air plus gases from bacterial fermentation; under ~1% is odorous sulfur compounds; intestinal gases such as hydrogen are absorbed into the blood and exhaled (the basis of hydrogen breath tests).
- **Shorter at night (ep 16):** intervertebral discs lose fluid under daytime compression; people are roughly 1 cm (often 1–2 cm) shorter in the evening and regain it lying down overnight. Astronauts' spines lengthen in microgravity (up to ~3%, about 5 cm), reversing after return (NASA).
- **Swallowing (ep 17):** the pharynx is shared by the airway and food passage; during swallowing the epiglottis folds over the laryngeal inlet and the vocal folds close, causing a brief swallow apnea (~0.5–1 s). Adults swallow several hundred times a day. Aspiration triggers the cough reflex.
- **Blind spot (ep 18):** the optic disc, where the optic nerve leaves the retina, has no photoreceptors; each eye has a blind spot about 15° to the side of the centre of vision, and the brain fills it in (perceptual filling-in).
